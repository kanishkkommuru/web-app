3. DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION
A. Problem Addressed by the Invention
Every day, thousands of students in India and across the globe commute alone — walking to bus stops, taking public transport, moving between campuses or heading home late from coaching classes and libraries. This routine act has a quiet but mounting peril. Alarmingly common are assaults, stalking, harassment, snatching, road accidents and medical emergencies involving lone students, particularly young women, first year students in unfamiliar cities and students with disabilities.
The problem isn’t a lack of technology; it’s a lack of the right technology, built for the student context. There are three unsatisfactory classes of existing solutions:
• Panic apps that rely on the smartphone: Students have to unlock their phone, open an app and tap a button — a slow, obvious process that’s not always feasible in the middle of a real threat.
• Generic personal alarms: Make noise but don’t transmit any location data, don’t contact anyone and don’t provide any evidence for post-incident follow-up.
• Industrial lone-worker devices: Bulky, expensive, not in tune with student mobility patterns, and utterly unaware of the social and environmental context of a student’s daily life, for construction or mining workers.
None of these solutions — which already exist — solve the very specific problem of a student who: (a) is moving from multiple locations during the day; (b) is working in a semi-public space with inconsistent connectivity; (c) cannot know when a threat might arise; and (d) needs discreet, hands-free, instantaneous assistance without attracting attention to themselves. The gap is technological and contextual, in that no existing patented device has been designed considering simultaneously student-specific threat patterns, student mobility 
behavior
 and institutional safety coordination.
B. Objectives of the Invention
The following objectives define the scope and intent of 
GuardianBand
:
To design a compact, student-wearable safety device capable of detecting threats through passive biometric monitoring and active gesture recognition — without requiring the student to take any conspicuous action.
To establish a peer-to-peer mesh communication network among students wearing the device, enabling location relay and distress signal propagation even when cellular coverage is unavailable.
To integrate a context-aware AI engine that learns the student's routine travel patterns and flags anomalies — such as unexpected detours, prolonged stillness in an unusual location, or deviation from a safe zone — as potential emergencies.
To provide seamless institutional integration, allowing universities, colleges, or schools to maintain a real-time safety dashboard without invasive continuous tracking of students.
To incorporate a multi-modal alert system that simultaneously notifies pre-registered guardians, a peer buddy network, and — when confirmed — local emergency services, with automatic evidence capture.
To ensure the device functions as a standalone unit independent of smartphone connectivity, removing the single point of failure present in all existing solutions.
To make the device accessible, affordable, and culturally acceptable for Indian student demographics, with particular attention to female student safety in tier-2 and tier-3 cities.

D. Detailed Description of the Invention
GuardianBand
 is a multi-layered, student-centric wearable safety system comprising three tightly integrated components: the physical wearable device, the AI processing engine, and the institutional safety network layer. Together, these components form a closed-loop protection architecture that is entirely novel in its design philosophy and technical composition.
COMPONENT 1 — THE PHYSICAL DEVICE
The wearable takes the form of a smart wristband with a secondary detachable clip module, allowing it to be worn on the wrist, attached to a bag strap, clipped to clothing, or worn as a lanyard — adapting to the student's preference and cultural context. The device houses the following hardware subsystems:
Biometric Sensor Array: A photoplethysmography (PPG) sensor for continuous heart rate monitoring; a galvanic skin response (GSR) sensor for stress and fear detection through perspiration changes; a 6-axis inertial measurement unit (IMU) comprising a 3-axis accelerometer and 3-axis gyroscope for motion, fall, struggle, and gait analysis.
Environmental Sensors: An ambient light sensor for day/night context detection; a microphone with directional audio analysis for sudden loud sounds, screaming, or aggressive voice tone patterns — processed locally without cloud transmission.
Communication Modules: An embedded 
eSIM
 for direct LTE/4G cellular communication independent of any smartphone; a Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) 5.2 module for short-range peer device communication and optional phone pairing; an 802.15.4 radio for mesh networking with other 
GuardianBand
 devices; a GPS/GNSS module with offline caching for location logging even when network is unavailable.
Processing and Storage: An ARM Cortex-M33 microcontroller with a dedicated edge AI coprocessor (NPU) for on-device inference; 256 KB of on-chip SRAM for real-time processing; 4 MB of flash storage for offline event logging and model caching.
Power: A 400 
mAh
 lithium polymer battery providing 48 to 72 hours of continuous use with all sensors active; wireless Qi charging; a low-power mode that extends battery to 10 days in standby with periodic location pings.
User Interface: A single haptic feedback motor for silent alerts; a tri-
color
 LED indicator; and one physical capacitive button with gesture recognition — distinguishing between a tap, a double-tap, a long press, and a squeeze pattern used to send a covert SOS.
COMPONENT 2 — THE AI CONTEXT ENGINE (ACE)
The AI Context Engine is the heart of what makes 
GuardianBand
 genuinely novel. It runs as a hybrid intelligence system — with a lightweight inference model executing directly on the device's NPU, and a richer model running on the institutional cloud backend for pattern validation.
During a 
three to seven day
 calibration period, the ACE silently builds a personal baseline for each student by mapping their typical daily routes, travel times, heart rate ranges across different activities, gait signatures, and regular stop locations (home, campus, canteen, library). This baseline is never shared externally — it is stored in encrypted form on the device and the student's institutional account only.
Once calibrated, the ACE monitors four threat signals simultaneously:
Biometric Deviation: A sudden spike in heart rate or GSR beyond the student's personal threshold — not a fixed value, but contextually weighted (e.g., elevated heart rate during a jog is normal; the same reading while the student is standing still at 11 PM is flagged).
Geo-
Behavioral
 Anomaly: Deviation from known routes, entry into locations never visited before, or lingering in an unfamiliar place beyond a statistically plausible duration for that time of day.
Motion Pattern Disruption: Detection of struggle motion (rapid, erratic limb movement), a fall event, or an abrupt cessation of all movement (collapse or immobilization).
Audio Context: Detection of a scream, aggressive vocal tone, or a sudden loud impact — 
analyzed
 using a pre-trained model running locally, without any audio leaving the device.
When one signal is triggered, the system moves to Alert Level 1 — a soft haptic buzz to the student asking for a check-in confirmation. If the student confirms safety by tapping the device, no alert is escalated. If there is no response within 90 seconds, or if two or more signals trigger simultaneously, the system escalates automatically to Alert Level 2 — notifying the student's registered buddy and guardian. At Alert Level 3 (triggered by a manual SOS gesture, three simultaneous threat signals, or no response to Level 2 within three minutes), the system contacts the institutional safety desk and initiates a voice call to the first registered emergency contact while sharing a live location link.
COMPONENT 3 — PEER MESH SAFETY NETWORK (PMSN)
The Peer Mesh Safety Network is a wholly original contribution of this invention with no equivalent in any prior art. When a 
GuardianBand
 device detects that it has lost cellular connectivity — whether due to a dead zone, a tunnel, or a network outage — it automatically activates its 802.15.4 mesh radio. Any other 
GuardianBand
 device within approximately 100 meters becomes a relay node.
If a student in distress is in a basement parking lot, a remote college building, or a rural area with no signal, their SOS is relayed hop-by-hop through other students' devices until it reaches a device with cellular connectivity, which then transmits the emergency to the backend. Crucially, relay devices do not reveal the content of the message or the identity of the sender to their wearers — only the destination server receives the decrypted SOS. The relay is fully encrypted and anonymous from the perspective of intermediate nodes. This design makes 
GuardianBand
 the first student safety device to implement anonymous cooperative emergency relay in a wearable form factor.
COMPONENT 4 — INSTITUTIONAL SAFETY DASHBOARD (ISD)
GuardianBand
 integrates with a web-based dashboard provided to educational institutions. The dashboard does not show continuous real-time tracking of all students — a feature deliberately omitted to protect student privacy and prevent the device from being used as a surveillance tool. Instead, the dashboard activates for a specific student only when an Alert Level 2 or Level 3 event is triggered, showing the last known location, the triggering event type, and the escalation history. Institutions can configure geofenced safe zones (campus boundary, approved hostel routes), outside which late-night travel by a registered student automatically prompts a soft check-in notification from the device.
E. Results and Advantages
GuardianBand
 demonstrates the following measurable advantages over existing prior art:
Hands-free and phone-free operation: Unlike all Bluetooth-dependent devices, 
GuardianBand
 requires no smartphone proximity. It functions as a fully autonomous safety device.
Predictive rather than reactive alerting: The ACE identifies distress signatures before a student is able to manually press a button — addressing the critical gap where victims are unable to act.
False alarm reduction through personalized baselines: By learning each student's individual patterns rather than applying fixed biometric thresholds, the system reduces false positives by an estimated 60 to 70 percent compared to generic motion-triggered devices.
Connectivity resilience via peer mesh: The PMSN ensures functional alert delivery in environments where no other device can operate, including underground transit, rural campuses, and cellular dead zones.
Privacy-by-design architecture: Continuous tracking is disabled by default; audio is processed on-device without cloud transmission; mesh relay is anonymized. This makes the device ethically deployable in student environments without consent concerns.
Cultural adaptability: The discreet form factor, the covert squeeze-SOS gesture, and the absence of any visible indicator during an alert allow students — particularly women in contexts where displaying distress may escalate a threat — to summon help silently.
Institutional coordination without surveillance: The privacy-gated dashboard gives institutions meaningful safety data at the moment it matters, without enabling routine monitoring of student movements.
F. Scope of Expansion
The following variables and extensions are considered within the protective scope of this invention:
Form factor variants: The invention is not limited to a wristband. The sensor and communication assembly may be embedded in a clip, lanyard badge, student ID card holder, earring stud, ring, or within school uniform fabric using textile electronics.
Platform extensions: The institutional dashboard may be extended to municipal transport authorities, hostel wardens, and parents through a tiered access model.
Sensor additions: Future variants may incorporate UV exposure sensors, air quality sensors for detecting chemical threats, or ultrasonic proximity sensors for detecting a person approaching from behind.
AI model expansion: The ACE threat model may be updated over-the-air to incorporate new 
behavioral
 signatures as threat patterns evolve, without hardware replacement.
Geographic scalability: While designed for the Indian student context, the device and network architecture are applicable globally, with localization for emergency service numbers, language-based audio alerts, and regional network protocols.
G. Working Prototype / Design Status
A functional proof-of-concept prototype has been developed using the following components as a validation platform:
ESP32-S3 microcontroller with dual-core processor and integrated Wi-Fi/BLE (serving as the development proxy for the target ARM Cortex-M33 + NPU platform)
MAX30102 pulse oximetry and heart rate sensor
MPU-6050 six-axis IMU (accelerometer + gyroscope)
SIM800L GSM/GPRS module for cellular communication
Neo-6M GPS module with offline NMEA logging
Custom Python-based edge AI model (TensorFlow Lite) for gesture and anomaly classification
Android companion app for alert visualization and buddy registration (optional pairing only)
The prototype has successfully demonstrated: autonomous SOS triggering on fall detection, location alert delivery via cellular without smartphone pairing, heart rate anomaly flagging against a personal baseline, and BLE-based relay between two prototype units in a no-cellular test environment.
A fully miniaturized wearable prototype with custom PCB design is estimated to be completed within six months from the date of this filing.
